# Author Voice, Humanization, and Non-Defensive Narrative

## Contents

- purpose, priority, and rewrite mode;
- preservation manifest and publishable center;
- narrative emphasis, negative results, and defensive writing;
- author voice and formulaic-prose diagnosis;
- section adaptation, verification, and reporting.

## Purpose and priority

Use this guide to make scholarly prose sound authored, specific, and persuasive while preserving the research record. Humanization is not detector evasion, synonym replacement, or removal of every hedge. Apply priorities in this order:

1. research integrity, complete reporting, and binding policy;
2. verified claims, evidence, and protected document elements;
3. a clear hierarchy centered on the strongest defensible contribution;
4. venue, language, disciplinary convention, and author voice;
5. surface naturalness and stylistic preference.

If a more fluent sentence changes any higher-priority item, reject the sentence.

## Select the rewrite mode

Determine the permitted intervention before editing:

- **Surface humanization:** preserve claim order, paragraph functions, and document structure; revise diction, syntax, rhythm, transitions, and local redundancy only.
- **Paragraph reconstruction:** preserve section logic but merge, split, or rebuild paragraphs around one controlling function.
- **Narrative reconstruction:** re-rank contributions, reorder sections or results, and rebuild the paper-level storyline within the verified record.
- **Review only:** diagnose formulaic prose, defensive framing, and evidence risks without changing files.

Do not silently escalate from surface editing to narrative reconstruction. When permission is unclear, make the smallest intervention that solves the request and list structural opportunities separately.

## Lock the research record first

Create or reuse a preservation manifest before substantial rewriting:

`item | source locator | protected form/meaning | allowed operation | verification`

Protect as applicable:

- numbers, signs, units, intervals, sample counts, effect directions, and significance status;
- primary and secondary endpoints, estimands, cohorts, splits, baselines, and analysis status;
- equations, symbols, assumptions, theorem scope, and algorithm behavior;
- citation keys, citation meaning, quotation wording, labels, cross-references, and custom macros;
- contribution boundaries, study chronology, prespecified versus exploratory status, and material limitations;
- bilingual technical terms and any author-approved terminology lock.

Do not invent a missing locator or smooth over disagreement between manuscript prose and artifacts. Resolve it from the source of truth or mark it unresolved.

## Find the publishable center

Treat the manuscript as a focused scholarly case, not a project summary or laboratory log.

1. Write one sentence for the problem the reader should care about.
2. Write one sentence for the precise limitation in current knowledge or practice.
3. Write one sentence for the paper's strongest defensible answer.
4. Identify the minimum evidence chain that makes that answer credible.
5. Assign every other result one role: explanation, validation, boundary, implementation, or secondary context.

Lead with the strongest contribution supported by the complete record. Do not distribute attention evenly across every experiment merely because it was performed. Chronology belongs in a provenance record when needed, not automatically in the manuscript narrative.

The central contribution may be a mechanism distinction, a fair evaluation design, a bounded negative finding, a reusable estimator, an interface correction, a dataset/resource, or a clinical decision insight. It need not be a universally superior model.

## Sharpen the story without selecting the record

Narrative emphasis is allowed; selective reporting is not.

- Keep every prespecified primary outcome and declared test set visible, regardless of direction.
- Keep a result that contradicts the headline, changes safety or fairness, exposes leakage, or materially narrows applicability.
- Keep uncertainty and the comparison needed to interpret an effect.
- Do not replace an unfavorable primary metric with a favorable secondary metric.
- Do not choose a baseline, subgroup, threshold, dataset, or visualization after seeing results unless the analysis is clearly labeled exploratory or post hoc.
- Do not turn representation, metric, or task selection into a retrospective claim of prior intent.
- Do not crop axes, omit intervals, change precision asymmetrically, or visually hide a comparator.

It is legitimate to reduce detail for a redundant exploratory dead end that neither tests a declared question nor affects interpretation, subject to venue and reporting requirements. Preserve it in the audit trail or supplement when completeness or reproducibility requires it.

## Handle negative results as evidence

Classify an unfavorable or null result before deciding placement:

- **Central contradiction:** conflicts with the headline or primary hypothesis. Report prominently and revise the claim.
- **Mechanism or applicability boundary:** shows where, when, or why the effect changes. Integrate it into the main story as a boundary finding.
- **Required integrity evidence:** concerns leakage, external testing, calibration, safety, fairness, subgroup performance, robustness, or a prespecified endpoint. Keep it in the appropriate main or supplementary location; do not demote it because it is unfavorable.
- **Secondary null:** does not overturn the central claim but informs scope. Report concisely with uncertainty.
- **Exploratory dead end:** has no bearing on a declared claim and is not required for complete reporting. It may be omitted from the narrative, but not misrepresented as unperformed.

Use factual language such as “did not improve,” “was lower,” or “the interval included zero.” Avoid emotional labels such as “failed badly,” apology, or speculation offered merely to rescue the result. If the design does not identify a mechanism, write “is consistent with,” “may reflect,” or a clearly labeled hypothesis.

## Remove defensive writing

Delete or rewrite language that performs no scientific function:

- generic apologies, ritual self-criticism, and lists of every conceivable weakness;
- repeated “although” or “despite” clauses that subordinate the contribution before it is stated;
- claims weakened only because an unrelated ideal study was not conducted;
- reviewer-facing meta-language such as “we acknowledge that our method is not perfect”;
- future-work promises used to excuse a missing requirement in the current claim.

Retain a limitation when it affects internal validity, bias, uncertainty, reproducibility, generalization, fairness, safety, clinical interpretation, authorship, or compliance. State the affected claim and resulting boundary precisely. If a limitation makes the headline unsupported, narrow the headline; do not bury the limitation.

Confident writing means matching sentence force to evidence. It does not mean replacing “did not improve” with “showed competitive potential,” or using “trade-off” without measuring both sides.

## Recover the author's voice

When author-approved samples are available, derive a compact voice profile from two to five representative passages, preferably accepted papers or text the author explicitly identifies as their own. Do not treat the target draft itself as a reliable voice sample unless the author confirms that it is representative. Record:

- first-person versus impersonal preference;
- typical claim directness and hedge strength;
- sentence-length variation and clause density;
- paragraph openings and transition style;
- technical term, abbreviation, and notation choices;
- punctuation, list, parenthesis, and citation-placement habits;
- Chinese information structure or English variety and disciplinary register.

Infer patterns, not phrases. Never copy distinctive wording, claims, examples, or citations from the style corpus. If no reliable sample exists, use clear field-appropriate prose and mark the voice profile as provisional rather than inventing personal habits.

## Diagnose formulaic academic prose

Revise patterns when they obscure the argument, but do not enforce a word blacklist or fixed sentence limit:

- generic scene setters that could introduce any paper;
- empty importance claims, inflated novelty, or vague actors;
- stacked transition adverbs and repeated conclusion sentences;
- overly symmetrical paragraphs, compulsory three-item lists, or identical sentence rhythms;
- noun-heavy abstractions where the responsible object or action can be named;
- strings of near-synonyms that destabilize technical terminology;
- citation clusters with no stated relationship to the claim;
- meta-writing such as “it is worth noting that” when the fact can be stated directly;
- repeated hedges whose scope is unclear;
- polished but content-free bridge sentences.

An em dash, passive construction, the verb “demonstrate,” a long sentence, or a repeated technical term may be correct. Judge function and local rhythm, not token presence. Do not force every paragraph to the same length or preserve paragraph count when reconstruction is authorized.

## Preserve necessary academic signals

- Keep established disciplinary terminology stable even when repetition is stylistically noticeable.
- Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or conventionally backgrounded; use active voice when agency matters.
- Keep hedges that encode uncertainty, scope, or causal limits; remove only vague or duplicated hedging.
- Keep citations adjacent to the claims they support and preserve each citation's semantic role.
- Keep formal definitions and mathematical statements exact; improve surrounding explanation rather than paraphrasing away precision.
- Preserve LaTeX commands, environments, math, `\label`, `\ref`, `\cite`, custom macros, table structure, and protected line breaks unless the user authorizes structural edits and verification is possible.

## Adapt by section

- **Title:** name the scientific object and strongest supported distinction; do not advertise aspiration as achievement.
- **Abstract:** foreground problem, answer, decisive evidence, and bounded implication. Do not spend scarce space on generic disclaimers, but include any boundary required to interpret the headline.
- **Introduction:** create need before detail, state the exact gap, reveal the response plainly, and make the contribution hierarchy explicit.
- **Results:** organize by research question or evidentiary role, not run order. Report effect and uncertainty before mechanism speculation.
- **Discussion:** interpret the strongest findings, explain what each control isolates, integrate boundary evidence, and concentrate material limitations rather than scattering apologies.
- **Conclusion:** reinforce the one memory point supported by the record; do not reopen a limitation inventory or introduce a larger claim.
- **Rebuttal/response:** answer directly, provide evidence, state the exact manuscript change, and distinguish clarification from new analysis or claim revision. Do not agree reflexively with an inaccurate premise.
- **Cover letter/highlights:** make novelty and fit legible but never exceed the manuscript's verified claims or conceal submission history and required disclosures.

## Rewrite and verify

Use two separate passes:

1. **Argument and voice pass:** repair hierarchy, paragraph functions, specificity, rhythm, transitions, and author voice.
2. **Integrity pass:** compare claims, protected strings, citations, numbers, analysis labels, negative evidence, and limitations against the preservation manifest and source artifacts.

For file edits, run available compilation, reference, protected-string, and diff checks. Inspect generated PDF or rendered output when layout can change meaning. A clean compile is necessary but does not prove semantic preservation.

## Report the work

For a short surface-humanization request, return the clean revised text by default. Add only a material unresolved evidence or scope issue; do not attach a full audit checklist or change log unless the user asks for one.

Unless the user asks for clean text only, return:

1. the revised text or edited files;
2. the central narrative decision in one or two sentences;
3. a concise record of structural and voice changes;
4. unresolved evidence, policy, or authorship questions;
5. confirmation of protected-element and integrity checks.

Do not claim that prose is “human-written” or “undetectable.” Describe the concrete improvements: author-voice alignment, reduced formulaic phrasing, clearer argument hierarchy, and verified evidence preservation.
