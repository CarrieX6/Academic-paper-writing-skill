# Chinese Academic Writing

Use this reference when Chinese is the manuscript language or when a submission requires paired Chinese and English front matter. It supplies a Chinese-language layer for conference papers, journal articles, and Chinese doctoral dissertations. Combine it with the relevant venue or dissertation mode; it does not replace the shared argument, evidence, citation, or section rules.

## Establish the Chinese-language contract

Before drafting or revising, determine:

- whether the body is Chinese, English, or bilingual, and which language is authoritative if versions diverge;
- whether Chinese is Simplified or Traditional and whether an official terminology or character-set rule applies;
- the target conference, journal, university, graduate school, discipline, degree type, and submission stage;
- which bilingual elements are required: title, abstract, keywords, headings, figure/table captions, declarations, or metadata;
- the applicable official template, style guide, reference style, terminology standard, and word or character limits.

Treat these as independent choices. A Chinese manuscript is not necessarily governed by a Chinese national reference standard, and a Chinese doctoral dissertation may impose institution-specific rules that differ from a journal in the same field. Current official instructions override the defaults below.

## Write native Chinese arguments rather than translated English

Preserve the scientific proposition and evidence level, but recast the sentence around a natural Chinese information order. Do not translate clause by clause merely because the source draft is English.

- Put the discourse topic, contrast, condition, or question where the reader needs it; do not mechanically preserve an English subject-first order.
- Prefer a visible actor and action when they matter. Reduce repetitive shells such as “本文对……进行了研究”“该方法实现了对……的提升” when a direct verb is clearer.
- Convert stacked English nominalizations into actions, relations, or conditions. Do not remove a technical nominalization when it names a real construct.
- Break long pre-nominal modifier chains. State the main proposition first or move conditions, mechanisms, and qualifications into separate clauses.
- Use explicit logical relations where inference could otherwise be ambiguous, but do not begin every sentence with formulaic connectors such as “首先”“其次”“此外”“综上所述”.
- Avoid empty intensifiers and ceremonial claims such as “具有十分重要的理论意义和现实价值” unless the manuscript supplies the specific consequence and evidence.
- Prefer bounded scholarly verbs: “表明”“提示”“支持”“估计”“观察到”“与……一致”. Reserve “证明”“揭示机制”“实现临床获益” for evidence that supports those stronger claims.
- Preserve necessary repetition of an exact technical term. Do not replace it with ornamental synonyms that change scope or make referents uncertain.

Chinese prose need not imitate either bureaucratic prose or conversational speech. Aim for explicit propositions, recoverable referents, controlled sentence depth, and stable terminology. A short sentence is not automatically clearer, and a long sentence is acceptable when its hierarchy remains unambiguous.

## Control referents and logical scope

Chinese permits subject omission, but omission is unsafe when adjacent sentences involve multiple datasets, methods, cohorts, groups, or causal agents.

- Restore the noun or a uniquely identifying short name when “其”“该”“这”“它” could refer to more than one antecedent.
- Keep the scope of negation, comparison, condition, and exception explicit. Distinguish “未观察到证据” from “证明不存在”.
- State the denominator, population, time point, comparison target, and uncertainty for consequential quantitative claims.
- Avoid switching between “模型”“方法”“系统”“框架” unless the referent genuinely changes.
- Use parallel grammar for parallel claims and matched comparison dimensions for contrasts.

Apply the shared paragraph-level argument rules from the main skill. Do not impose a uniquely Chinese paragraph count, sentence count, or mandatory four-part structure.

## Maintain a bilingual terminology ledger

For bilingual work, track at least:

`concept | preferred Chinese | preferred English | abbreviation/symbol | first-use form | authoritative source | prohibited variants`

Then enforce the following:

1. Prefer an official Chinese term from an applicable standard, authoritative professional body, canonical Chinese edition, dataset owner, or method author when one exists.
2. When no settled translation exists, choose a semantically accurate rendering, give the English term at first use when useful, and keep the choice stable. Mark disputed or provisional translations internally.
3. Preserve official names of datasets, models, software, organizations, trials, instruments, and registered endpoints. Translate or transliterate them only when the owner or target guide supplies an accepted form.
4. Define each abbreviation in the language required by the manuscript and template. Do not repeatedly expand familiar abbreviations or create an abbreviation used only once.
5. Keep mathematical symbols, labels, class names, and task definitions identical across Chinese prose, equations, figures, tables, supplements, and the English abstract.
6. Distinguish near-neighbors rather than flattening them in translation—for example, association versus causation, validation versus verification, efficacy versus effectiveness, and uncertainty versus variability.

Do not translate a term merely to avoid English. In technical Chinese, a stable English name may be less ambiguous than an invented Chinese calque.

## Apply punctuation and mixed-script typography consistently

Follow the official template or publisher typesetting specification first. Otherwise use these conservative defaults:

- Use Chinese full-width punctuation for Chinese sentence structure and ASCII punctuation inside English text, code, paths, URLs, identifiers, and mathematical expressions.
- Keep paired marks matched and nested clearly. Use Chinese quotation marks and title marks only where their semantic function is appropriate; do not manually restyle bibliography titles against the required reference style.
- Do not use an English comma or colon to carry the grammar of a Chinese sentence solely because an adjacent token is English.
- Keep hyphens, minus signs, en/em dashes, ranges, and negative signs semantically distinct, especially in formulas, confidence intervals, dates, and model names.
- Preserve official capitalization and internal punctuation in acronyms, gene or protein symbols, product names, datasets, and software.
- Choose a consistent policy for spaces between Chinese characters and Latin letters or Arabic numerals. If the template is silent, preserve the document's established professional typography rather than applying a universal spacing rule mechanically.
- Prevent line-level edits from changing LaTeX commands, citation keys, equation notation, cross-references, code, or machine-readable metadata.

Inspect the compiled PDF or final rendered document. Source-level consistency does not guarantee correct line breaks, punctuation placement, font fallback, emphasis, or equation alignment.

## Standardize numbers, units, dates, and statistical notation

- Follow the target's current instructions and the applicable scientific or disciplinary standard for quantities and units; use SI conventions unless the field or reporting requirement specifies otherwise.
- Use Arabic numerals for measured quantities, sample sizes, statistical results, model settings, and ordered labels unless the official style requires another form.
- Keep a value and its unit, range, sign, decimal precision, and uncertainty together and semantically stable. Do not convert units or round values during prose revision without authorization and traceable source data.
- Use one notation for percentages, intervals, significance values, decimal markers, multiplication signs, and ranges throughout the manuscript, subject to field-specific rules.
- Give dates in an unambiguous order and follow the required Chinese or international format. Preserve registered study dates and database field formats exactly where necessary.
- Do not translate decimal points, equation punctuation, variable names, or software parameter syntax into full-width characters.

Typography around units, percentages, temperature, angles, and ranges varies across standards and templates. Verify the applicable rule instead of encoding a remembered convention as universal.

## Align Chinese and English front matter by claim

When paired titles, abstracts, or keywords are required, treat them as two controlled expressions of the same scientific record, not as a literal translation exercise.

### Titles

- Preserve the same population or domain, intervention or method, task, study design, and claim strength in both languages.
- Prefer a concise idiomatic title in each language; word order may differ, but scientific scope may not.
- Use official bilingual names for institutions, datasets, consortia, and study designs when available.

### Abstracts

- Map each consequential element across versions: motivation, objective or question, design/data, method, primary evidence, uncertainty where material, conclusion, and limitation if required.
- Keep sample sizes, cohort counts, endpoints, metrics, numerical results, confidence intervals, directions of effect, and qualifiers identical.
- Do not add a contribution, causal interpretation, clinical claim, or limitation to only one language version.
- Follow the required structured or unstructured format independently in each language; headings and length need not be mirror images unless the official guide requires that.

### Keywords

- Use the required number and controlled vocabulary where the venue, discipline, or indexing service specifies one.
- Select Chinese and English terms that retrieve the same concepts; do not translate a broad Chinese keyword into a narrower English one or vice versa.
- Keep keyword capitalization, separators, and ordering consistent with the template.

Run a bilingual claim audit after either version changes. Sentence-by-sentence identity is unnecessary; claim-by-claim equivalence is mandatory.

## Handle Chinese citations and references without assuming one style

The official venue, university, or graduate-school rule determines citation and bibliography style. Do not impose GB/T 7714 merely because the manuscript is Chinese. If GB/T 7714 is required, verify the required edition, citation system, document-type rules, and any local institutional modifications from the current official source; do not rely on memory or a generic BibTeX style name.

- Use the official template, bibliography style, CSL, or institution-provided example when available; do not hand-format each entry unless required.
- Keep narrative and parenthetical/numeric citations grammatical in Chinese, but let the mandated citation system determine author display, punctuation, ordering, and locators.
- Place a citation where its support scope is clear. Avoid attaching one reference to a long sentence containing several claims it does not all support.
- Preserve source-language titles and author names unless the required style explicitly asks for translation, transliteration, or bilingual metadata.
- Never invent an English title for a Chinese work. If an official English title exists, verify it from the publisher, journal, repository record, DOI metadata, or the work itself.
- Distinguish Chinese and non-Chinese versions, translations, reprints, standards, dissertations, conference records, preprints, and final archival publications.
- Keep Chinese author-name order and romanization faithful to the authoritative record; do not infer given/family-name boundaries from appearance alone.

### Verify Chinese-language sources

Search engines, bibliographic databases, institutional repositories, and aggregators may discover records, but a consequential citation should be checked against the strongest available record:

1. the article, book, standard, dissertation, dataset documentation, or official report itself;
2. the publisher, journal, standards body, university repository, conference, or responsible institution;
3. DOI or another authoritative metadata registry where applicable.

Record the source's exact Chinese title, authors, publication status, year, venue or degree-granting institution, stable locator, and the passage supporting the claim. Check for duplicate Chinese/English records, withdrawn or corrected versions, unofficial reposts, and metadata corrupted by character conversion. Index inclusion alone does not establish scientific quality or support the cited claim.

## Audit the Chinese language layer

In addition to the shared scientific and structural audit, confirm:

1. The manuscript's Chinese variety, technical register, and terminology policy are explicit and consistent.
2. Each key concept has one stable Chinese-English-symbol mapping, with official names protected.
3. No sentence preserves English syntax at the expense of clear Chinese information structure.
4. Pronouns, omitted subjects, comparisons, negations, conditions, and qualifiers have unambiguous scope.
5. Chinese/Latin/numeric typography, punctuation, units, dates, and statistical notation render correctly in the final document.
6. Chinese and English titles, abstracts, and keywords make equivalent claims and contain identical consequential facts.
7. The required citation and bibliography standard—not a presumed national default—is applied from a current official source.
8. Chinese-language references are real, retrievable, correctly encoded, and verified at the claim level.
9. Automated translation or language polishing has not altered numbers, symbols, citations, cohort definitions, endpoint meanings, evidence strength, or limitations.
10. The final PDF has been visually inspected for Chinese font fallback, missing glyphs, punctuation at line boundaries, table wrapping, caption alignment, bookmarks, and searchable/extractable text when required.

When an official rule is unavailable or ambiguous, record the chosen convention and apply it consistently. Do not present a house convention as a universal Chinese academic-writing rule.
